# Psalm 1: Two Ways of Being Human

## Overview

This sermon explores Psalm 1 as a reflection on two different ways of being human—one that leads to flourishing life and one that leads to withering ruin. Through the teachings of Tim and Jon from BibleProject, we discover that the key difference between these two paths is what we choose to meditate on. The good life comes from delighting in and meditating on God's instruction day and night, which plants us like a tree by streams of water, bearing fruit in season.

## When to Follow This Sermon

- When you need to understand what the "blessed" or "good life" really means in the biblical context
- When facing choices between the three destructive patterns (walking in the counsel of the wicked, standing in the path of sinners, sitting in the seat of mockers) and the life-giving practice of meditating on God's instruction
- When seeking to build a habit of meditating on Scripture rather than being shaped by the counsel of the wicked, the path of sinners, and the seat of mockers
- When you want to grasp the meaning of key Hebrew terms like *ashrey*, *barukh*, *torah*, and *haga*
- When teaching others about the practical outworking of Psalm 1 in daily life

## Steps

### Step 1: Recognize the Three Destructive Patterns

Identify the progression of choices that lead away from the good life: walking in the counsel of the wicked, standing in the path of sinners, and sitting in the seat of mockers. Notice how this progression moves from movement toward becoming stuck. The wicked are morally backwards, sinners miss the target of loving God and neighbor, and mockers have become so jaded they can only show contempt for goodness.

### Step 2: Choose the Pathway of Meditation

Instead of following the destructive patterns, choose to delight in the instruction of Yahweh (*torah*). Meditate on it day and night. Understand that delight and meditation form an infinity loop—the more you delight in God's instruction, the more you will meditate on it, and the more you meditate, the more you will delight.

### Step 3: Practice Biblical Meditation

Learn the Hebrew concept of meditation (*haga*), which means quietly reciting the words of Scripture aloud or in your mind to focus your attention so that these words become part of you. This is not emptying your mind, but filling it with God's wisdom through slow, repeated reflection.

### Step 4: Bear Fruit Like a Tree

Allow this meditation to transform you into a tree planted by streams of water—firmly rooted, productive in its season, and never withering. Recognize that this imagery echoes the Garden of Eden and represents becoming a source of abundant life for yourself and others.

### Step 5: Avoid the Chaff Destiny

Remember the contrast: the wicked are like chaff blown away by the wind—empty, unstable, and unable to stand when God brings judgment, while the meditator who avoids the path of sinners will be able to stand.

## Examples

### Example 1: The Tree and the Chaff

Psalm 1 presents two vivid images: the meditator is like a tree planted by streams of water that gives fruit in its time and whose leaf never withers. In contrast, the wicked person is like chaff—an empty husk of wheat that flakes off and blows away in the wind. One is firmly rooted with deep water access; the other has nothing to hold it steady when the winds of judgment come.

### Example 2: The Progression of Becoming Stuck

The poem describes a progression from walking, to standing, to sitting. Walking by the counsel of the wicked is the first movement away from God. Standing in the path of sinners shows a slowing down and becoming established in wrong patterns. Sitting in the seat of mockers represents total entrenchment—becoming so jaded that contempt for goodness becomes one's permanent posture.

### Example 3: The Infinity Loop of Delight and Meditation

The structure of Psalm 1:2 creates a beautiful symmetry: "His delight is in the instruction of Yahweh, and on his instruction, he meditates day and night." Delight and meditation fuel each other in an infinity loop. What you delight in, you meditate on. What you meditate on, you increasingly delight in. This is the mechanism by which God's wisdom becomes part of your identity.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Meditate on Scripture daily, both morning and evening, allowing God's words to become part of you
- ✅ Delight in God's instruction rather than treating it as a duty—let enjoyment drive your meditation
- ✅ Recognize the slow shaping power of your choices—small decisions compound over time into either rooted strength or aimless drifting
- ✅ Identify and avoid the three destructive patterns before they progress from walking to sitting
- ✅ Let meditation on God's word be the pathway to becoming a source of life and fruit for others

- ❌ Don't treat meditation as emptying your mind—biblical meditation (*haga*) means filling it with Scripture through recitation and reflection
- ❌ Don't underestimate the progression of compromise—walking with the wicked, then standing with sinners, then sitting with mockers
- ❌ Don't confuse experiencing God's favor (*barukh*) with merely being spoken well of by others (*ashrey*)
- ❌ Don't miss the Eden imagery—meditation on God's instruction is meant to restore the flourishing life humanity was created for

## Keep In Mind

- This sermon does not replace personal study of the biblical text, engagement with the Holy Spirit, or community accountability in spiritual formation.
- The Hebrew words ashrey, barukh, torah, and haga carry layers of meaning that go beyond simple English translations—*ashrey* is about the community recognizing someone's good life, not just individual happiness.
- Meditation is a lifetime practice, not a quick fix. The poem is "designed for a lifetime of meditation, reading and re-reading slowly, allowing it to guide all of our choices."
- The contrast between the two paths is ultimately about what controls your attention and shapes your character over time.
- This teaching is rooted in the biblical text of Psalm 1 and reflects traditional Jewish and Christian interpretation of the psalm.
- No physical, financial, or digital security risks are associated with this sermon content.
- The concepts presented are spiritual and ethical in nature, intended for personal formation and community teaching.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Reading "blessed is the man" as a promise of individual happiness rather than a community-observed good life rooted in God's favor.
  **Solution:** Remember the two Hebrew words: *barukh* (experiencing God's favor) produces *ashrey* (what the community rightly says about that person's good life).

- **Problem:** Treating meditation as a mystical, mind-emptying practice disconnected from the actual words of Scripture.
  **Solution:** Embrace the Hebrew meaning of *haga*—quietly reciting, murmuring, or chewing on the actual text of God's instruction until it becomes part of you.

- **Problem:** Missing the seriousness of the progression from walking to standing to sitting, thinking small compromises don't matter.
  **Solution:** See each step as a deeper entrenchment—every choice to walk with the wicked, stand with sinners, or sit with mockers shapes your character and destiny.

- **Problem:** Reading the tree and chaff imagery as mere poetic decoration rather than the central metaphor for two whole ways of being human.
  **Solution:** Let the contrast sink in: one person is deeply rooted with constant water access bearing fruit; the other is light, empty, and at the mercy of every wind. Your meditation determines which one you become.
