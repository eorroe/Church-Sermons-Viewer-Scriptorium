# How to Spot Religious Hypocrisy in Yourself

## Overview

This sermon from BibleProject teaches that the truly good life flows out of a close, intimate relationship with the Creator of the universe. Jesus taught that doing righteousness is our response to receiving God's love, and that love is then given back out to others. However, Jesus warns against the temptation to turn intimacy with God into a performance designed to gain public praise. Using the three most common ways Jewish people expressed devotion to God—generosity, prayer, and fasting—Jesus reveals how even good practices can become stage acts for approval. He calls followers to cultivate a genuine, private relationship with God, trusting that the Father who sees what is done in secret will reward them.

## When to Follow This Sermon

- When you sense your spiritual practices becoming routine or performative rather than heartfelt
- When you struggle with the desire for public recognition or praise for your good deeds
- When you want to examine your motives behind generosity, prayer, or fasting
- When you feel pressure to appear more devout or righteous than you actually are
- When you are learning to build a more authentic, intimate relationship with God
- When you need wisdom on balancing private devotion with public witness

## Steps

### Step 1: Understand the Warning Against Performative Righteousness

Jesus warns, "Be careful that you don't do your righteousness in front of people for the purpose of being seen by them. If you do, you will have no reward from your Father in the skies." Acting like you are close with God might gain you social status or applause, but Jesus says that is all you will get. He describes this performance as being a "hypocrite," from the Greek word *hupokrites*, meaning a stage actor putting on a show. The first step is recognizing the temptation to perform righteousness, which robs you of the reward of knowing and being known by God.

### Step 2: Examine Your Generosity

Jesus says, "When you give generously to those in need, don't signal it with a trumpet as the hypocrites do in the town squares or streets. They do that to be honored by people. And I tell you the truth, they have fully received their reward." In Jesus' culture, public displays of generosity were a way to celebrate God's love, but they could also promote yourself. Jesus offers an alternative: "When you give generously to those in need, don't let your left hand know what your right hand is doing so that your giving is done privately, and your Father who sees what is done in private, he will fully reward you." One way to make sure your generosity is really done out of love is to do it in ways other people cannot see. However, Jesus also taught to let your light and good deeds shine as an example for others. This is wisdom, not a rule. It forces you to cultivate a closer relationship with God, trusting that He will show you when to give in private or more publicly.

### Step 3: Evaluate Your Prayer Life

Jesus says, "And when you pray, don't be like the hypocrites, who love to pray while standing where people gather or on street corners so that they can be seen by people. I tell you the truth, they have fully received their reward." In Jesus' culture, most Jewish people stopped whatever they were doing, throughout the day, and prayed a traditional prayer from the Torah called the Shema. Jesus himself was known for his consistent devotion to prayer. But be careful, because even your prayer habits can become a strategy for public praise. Jesus suggests: "But when you pray, go into a room, shut the door, so you can pray to your Father in private. And your Father who can see what's done in private, he will reward you." Prayer is about spending time with God, sharing your thoughts and concerns, and trusting that He will hear and respond. One way to check your motives for praying is to do it when no one can see. Again, this is wisdom, not a rule. Jesus prayed alone and also together with his followers. Both are important ways of connecting to God.

### Step 4: Check Your Fasting Practices

Jesus says, "When you all fast, don't look gloomy like the hypocrites. They make their faces look disfigured, so their fasting will be seen by others. I tell you the truth, they have received their reward." Fasting is a way of praying with your body, by withholding your own physical needs, like food and drink, and then inviting God's presence to meet you in that place of need. But even fasting can be used as a performance. Jesus offers the alternative: "But when you fast, anoint your head with oil, wash your face, so your fasting isn't visible to anyone except your Father in private, and he will reward you." Notice that in all three cases, Jesus does not say to ditch these practices. Express your devotion, but find creative ways to do it so that you do not treat God's love like a trophy.

### Step 5: Cultivate a Relationship Over Reputation

Jesus knows that his followers will be most effective in the world when they care the least about their social standing, because their ultimate goal is simply to know and be known by the God of generous love. The goal is not to earn God's love through perfect performance, but to respond to the love you have already received. When you are rooted in that intimacy, your generosity, prayer, and fasting flow naturally—not for applause, but as a genuine expression of who you are becoming in Christ.

## Examples

### Example 1: The Trumpet and the Secret Gift

In Jesus' day, some wealthy people would hire a trumpet player to announce their charitable donations in the town square. The "reward" they received was the admiration of the crowd. Today, this might look like posting every act of kindness on social media, mentioning your tithe in conversation, or volunteering only when there is a camera present. The alternative Jesus gives is private, unpublicized giving—where the only audience is God.

### Example 2: The Street Corner Prayer

A person might choose a busy street corner or a visible seat in a prayer room precisely because others will see them praying. The external appearance of devotion becomes the goal. Jesus' alternative is to enter a private room, shut the door, and pray to the Father in secret. This removes the audience and reveals whether the prayer is truly about communion with God or about maintaining a spiritual image.

### Example 3: The gloomy fast

Fasting was a common spiritual discipline in Jesus' time, but some people deliberately made their disfigurement from fasting obvious to others so they would receive praise for their piety. Jesus' instruction to anoint your head and wash your face means carrying on normally so that no one can tell you are fasting—except God. This protects the fast from becoming a performance and keeps the focus on drawing near to God in need.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Regularly audit your motives behind generosity, prayer, and fasting by asking: "Would I still do this if no one else ever found out?"
- ✅ Practice private spiritual disciplines as a way to deepen intimacy with God rather than to build a spiritual reputation
- ✅ Give generously and publicly when it genuinely serves and inspires others, but guard your heart against the praise that follows
- ✅ Pray both privately and in community, recognizing that each strengthens the other when the motive is love of God
- ✅ Use fasting as a way to pray with your body and invite God's presence, not as a spiritual competition
- ✅ Remember that Jesus' instructions are wisdom, not legalistic rules—ask God to show you when to practice privately and when to let your light shine
- ✅ Let your ultimate goal be knowing and being known by the God of generous love, rather than being known by others as a spiritual person

## Keep In Mind

- This sermon is about heart motives, not about banning public acts of devotion. Jesus himself prayed in public and gave publicly when it served others.
- The call to privacy is a safeguard against hypocrisy, not a prohibition on community worship or shared spiritual practices.
- Spiritual disciplines like generosity, prayer, and fasting are still commanded and encouraged; Jesus is warning against the corrupting motive of human praise.
- If you are unsure whether your motives are pure, choosing the private option is always the wiser path until you gain clarity.
- Seeking reward from God is not selfish—Jesus promises that the Father who sees in secret will reward you. The reward is relationship with Him.
- Experiencing performative spirituality does not make you a failure; it makes you human. Jesus gave these teachings precisely because the temptation is so common.
- This sermon addresses personal spiritual motives and does not require sharing sensitive personal information.
- Do not use this teaching to shame or judge others for their spiritual practices.
- If you experience scrupulosity or compulsive religious behavior, seek guidance from a trusted spiritual director or mental health professional.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Doing generosity, prayer, or fasting primarily to impress others and gain social status
  **Solution:** Test your motives by asking whether you would still do the act if it were completely anonymous. If not, practice it privately until your heart aligns with love for God rather than love of praise.
- **Problem:** Using Jesus' teaching about letting your light shine as an excuse to publicize every spiritual act
  **Solution:** Remember that letting your light shine is about reflecting God's character in the world, not about advertising your own spirituality. Let your life and consistent character speak before you speak about your own deeds.
- **Problem:** Becoming legalistic about doing everything in private, missing the value of community and public witness
  **Solution:** Jesus prayed both alone and with his followers. Both private and communal practices have value. Ask God for wisdom to know when each is appropriate rather than creating a new rule.
- **Problem:** Treating spiritual disciplines as a checklist or a way to earn God's love
  **Solution:** Righteousness flows out of relationship, not performance. Your generosity, prayer, and fasting should be responses to God's love, not attempts to buy it. Return to the simplicity of knowing and being known by God.
